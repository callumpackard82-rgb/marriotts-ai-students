1 Why this matters

Here's the quiet revolution nobody put on a poster: plain English is now a programming language. People describe an app — a revision quiz, a fixture tracker for their football team, a little game — and AI writes the code. Working software, built by people who couldn't code last year.

That doesn't make programming dead — it makes a different skill suddenly precious: knowing exactly what you want, saying it precisely, and testing whether you got it. Engineers have a word for people who can do that: engineers. This module is about thinking like one — a skill you can build right now, with or without ever touching an AI.

2 The idea

The builder's loop

Every AI-assisted project — every software project, honestly — runs on the same four-step loop:

1 · Describe Say exactly what you want — who it's for, what it does, what "done" looks like.
2 · Build The AI drafts it. Fast, confident, and — you know from E1 — not always right.
3 · Test You try to break it. Weird inputs, wrong clicks, empty boxes. Whatever a real user would do.
4 · Refine Report what broke, precisely. Repeat until it survives you.

Look at where the human is in that loop: steps 1, 3 and 4 — the deciding, the testing, the judging. The AI only owns step 2. That's the pilot's seat-map from E4, drawn for builders. Three habits separate real builders from passengers:

  • Specific beats vague, every time. "Make me a quiz app" gets you the average quiz app (E1's average machine, again). "Ten-question French vocab quiz, multiple choice, instant feedback, score at the end, works on a phone" gets you your app. The spec is the skill.
  • Understand what you ship. If AI wrote code you can't explain, you're a passenger in your own project — and the first bug will prove it. The builder's move: "explain every part until I could rebuild it." (T1's explain-it-back, applied to code.)
  • Report bugs like a scientist: "It's broken, fix it" gets guesses. "When I click submit with nothing selected, the score shows NaN — I expected an error message" gets fixes. Precision in, quality out.

3 See it in action

One weekend, one vocab quiz

Year 10 student, French test coming, wants a phone quiz of her actual vocab list. Watch the loop run:

  • Describe: "A quiz web page: I'll give you 20 French–English word pairs. Ask one at a time, multiple choice with 4 options, tell me instantly if I'm right, score at the end, big buttons for a phone." (Notice: someone who's done T1 even knows why the quiz beats re-reading the list.)
  • Build: the AI produces a working page in one go. Looks perfect. Looks.
  • Test: she tries to break it — and does, twice. Answering super-fast double-counts the score; one French word appears as its own wrong-answer option. A passenger would have shipped both bugs.
  • Refine: two precise bug reports later, it's solid. Then one more loop: "add a 'practise my mistakes' round at the end" — because she knows (T1 again) that's where the learning is.

Total code she wrote: zero. Total engineering she did: all of it. The spec, the test plan, the bug reports, the feature that made it actually useful — none of that was in the machine.

4 Your turn

Builder duels: which move builds better?

Five head-to-heads. Pick the move a real builder makes.

Duel 1 · The opening prompt
🔧 B. A is a wish; B is a spec — who it's for, what it does, what "working" means. Vague in, average out. And writing spec B forced her to decide what she actually wanted, which is half the engineering.
Duel 2 · The code appears and looks perfect
🔧 B. "It runs" and "it works" are different claims — E1 told you the machine is confident, not correct. Testing is the step where you add the quality. Builders are proud of the bugs they catch, because each one is a bug their users never met.
Duel 3 · It works, but you don't know how
🔧 A. Code you can't explain is code you can't fix, extend or trust. And here's the secret: asking "explain it to me" is what the professionals do with AI all day — it's not the beginner move, it's the move. (B is the fluency trap from T1 wearing a hoodie.)
Duel 4 · Something's broken
🔧 B. What happened, what you expected, what the evidence says — that's a bug report, and it's a thinking skill worth more than any syntax. A makes the AI guess; B makes it fix. (Notice this is just T3's precision applied to machines: vague questions get vague answers everywhere.)
Duel 5 · The bigger project
🔧 A. One giant wish produces one giant haystack of code nobody understands (including the AI). Piece by piece, tested as you go, means you always know what just broke: the last piece. Architects don't lay bricks — but they absolutely decide where the walls go. Stay the architect.

5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

Building's extra rules

  • Your projects inherit E2's rules. No real people's data in what you build or in your prompts — your quiz needs vocab words, not your friends' names. If an app you make collects anything from other people, stop and think hard (and don't collect it from under-13s at all).
  • Sharing a thing you built = publishing. Before your quiz/game/tool goes to the group chat, it gets the same checks as anything else you publish — that's the whole of C3, next.
  • Age rules apply to builder tools too — and there's zero shame in the no-AI path: Scratch, the school's CS lessons and code clubs build the identical thinking. The loop is the skill; the AI is one of several ways to run it.
  • The standing lines: no AI at school · nothing personal in the box. Computer Science homework also follows T2 — if AI wrote your coursework code, that's the same malpractice as an essay.
