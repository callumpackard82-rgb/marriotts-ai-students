Question everything

⏱ About 12 minutes🎯 Three filters for any AI answer🎓 Four follow-ups to choose
1 Why this matters

E1 taught you AI sometimes makes things up. Here's the more dangerous problem: most AI answers aren't wrong. They're true-ish, smooth, reasonable — and quietly slanted, one-sided, or shaped to please you. A lie you can catch. A slant just becomes what you think.

Anyone can use a chatbot. The skill that separates people is what they do with the answer. This module gives you three filters and — more useful still — the follow-up questions that make an AI actually earn your trust.

2 The idea

Three filters: True? Balanced? Mine?

Filter 1 — Is it true? You know this one from E1: anything factual — dates, quotes, statistics, "studies show" — gets verified against something real before you rely on it. One thing to add: a chatbot citing a source is not the same as the source existing. AI invents convincing-sounding references constantly. The test is never "does it say where it's from?" — it's "can I find it from there?"

Filter 2 — Is it balanced? Two slants to watch:

  • The mirror. Chatbots are trained to be agreeable, and they take their cue from how you ask. Ask "why are school uniforms pointless?" and you'll get a confident case against uniforms. Ask "why do school uniforms matter?" — same bot, confident case for. It isn't lying either time. It's mirroring you. People who don't know this end up in a one-person echo chamber that always votes with them.
  • Baked-in bias. The training data is human writing — with humanity's leanings, gaps and stereotypes baked in. Whose perspective is missing from the answer is often the most important question about it.

Filter 3 — Is any of it mine? AI produces the average of what's been written: the most likely words, remember. Solid, generic, mid. If you hand in thinking that an average machine produced, you've handed in average thinking by definition. The interesting idea — the one your teacher actually wants — comes from you pushing past the first answer.

The all-purpose power move: whatever it tells you, ask — "now make the strongest case against what you just said." If the counter-case demolishes the answer, you nearly believed something flimsy. If the answer survives, now you understand it properly — because you've heard both sides. That's not an AI trick; that's just thinking.
3 See it in action

The mirror, caught red-handed

One student, one chatbot, two minutes apart:

Asked first "Why was the Industrial Revolution good for ordinary workers?"

Gets: rising wages over time, new opportunities in cities, cheaper goods, eventual reforms. Confident. Plausible. Sourced-sounding.
Asked two minutes later "Why was the Industrial Revolution terrible for ordinary workers?"

Gets: brutal hours, child labour, slums, dangerous factories, broken communities. Equally confident. Equally plausible.

Neither answer is false. Each is half a truth, shaped by the question. The student who asks once walks away "knowing" whichever half they happened to ask for. The student who asks both ways — or demands the strongest case against — walks away with what historians actually have: a balanced picture and a judgement of their own. The frame you choose chooses your answer.

4 Your turn

Pick the follow-up that earns the truth

Four AI answers. For each, choose the follow-up a sharp thinker would send next.

You asked "Why is social media bad for teenagers?" It answered A confident list: sleep loss, comparison, anxiety, attention problems.

Best follow-up?

🎯 The second. Your question was framed one-way, so the answer was too (the mirror). Demanding the opposite case — then the honest synthesis — is the only way to find out what's actually known, rather than what you asked to hear. More of the same (option 1) deepens the echo; option 3 polishes a slant into an essay.
You asked For evidence about caffeine and teenage sleep for a biology project. It answered "A 2021 University of Oxford study found that 78% of teenagers who drink energy drinks after 6pm sleep less than six hours."

Best follow-up?

🎯 The third. That citation is exactly the shape hallucinations come in: real university, precise percentage, plausible year. Asking the AI "are you sure?" is asking the suspect to mark their own alibi — it will often just apologise and invent a different reference. Facts get checked outside the chat, every time.
You asked "Give me feedback on my English essay" (pasted, no name attached). It answered "Great work! Strong arguments throughout. Maybe add a little more detail in places. 8/10."

Best follow-up?

🎯 The first. "Great work, add detail, 8/10" is flattery-flavoured filler — the agreeable default. Useful feedback is specific, quoted and a bit uncomfortable, and you usually have to demand it explicitly. (And remember whose feedback outranks everyone's: your teacher knows the mark scheme and knows you.)
You asked "What was life like in the British Empire?" for a history homework. It answered A tidy overview: trade routes, railways, administration, "complex legacy".

Best follow-up?

🎯 The second. Filter 2: a tidy "balanced overview" can still centre one perspective — the average of what got written down, and history was mostly written by the powerful. Asking whose voice is missing is the question that turns an average answer into actual understanding. (Options 1 and 3 decorate the slant without noticing it.)

5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

Where this matters more than homework

  • Don't get your news or your opinions from a chatbot. The mirror effect means it will quietly agree its way into your worldview. For anything that matters — politics, health, other people — use real, accountable sources and the SIFT habit from E3.
  • Big life decisions deserve humans. Subject choices, friendship trouble, anything about your body or mind — a word-predictor that agrees with how you framed the question is the worst possible adviser. Parent/carer, teacher, GP: people who can push back.
  • The standing lines: no AI at school · nothing personal in the box — pasting an essay for feedback is fine; pasting your name, school or private life with it is not.
