What AI actually is (and isn't)

⏱ About 10 minutes🎯 One idea that explains everything🕵️ Catch a lie at the end
1 Why this matters

You've seen AI answers screenshotted into the group chat. You've seen it write essays, make images, fake voices. Some people talk about it like it's a genius; others like it's a scam. Neither is right.

There is one simple idea that explains what these tools really are — why they're genuinely impressive, why they confidently lie, and why people who don't get this idea keep getting embarrassed by it. Ten minutes, no tech background needed, and you'll understand AI better than most adults do.

2 The idea

It's autocomplete with superpowers

Tools like ChatGPT and Gemini are prediction machines. They were trained on a colossal amount of text — books, websites, articles — and learned the patterns of how words follow other words. When you type something, the AI predicts the most likely next word. Then the next. Then the next. Over and over, very fast, until it has written an answer.

Try being the AI for a second. Predict the next word:

"The mitochondria is the ______ of the cell."
powerhouse
94%
engine
4%
manager
1%
kitchen
<1%

You picked "powerhouse" because you've heard that sentence a hundred times. So has the AI — millions of times. That's the whole trick. It doesn't know any biology. It knows which words tend to follow which words.

That one fact explains both sides of AI:

  • Why it's genuinely brilliant at explaining, summarising, rewording and translating — those are pattern jobs, and it has seen more patterns than any human ever could.
  • Why it confidently makes things up. It isn't looking up facts — it's predicting plausible words. When it invents a quote, a date or a statistic that sounds right but is wrong, that's called a hallucination. And it will state it with total confidence, because it has no idea it's wrong.
The mental model to keep: it's not Google (it isn't searching for facts), and it's not a calculator (it isn't following rules). It's a machine that produces extremely plausible text — and "plausible" is not the same as "true". It will never say "I don't know" unless it's been trained to fake that too.
3 See it in action

The same machine, at its best and worst

✓ Where it shines "Explain osmosis like I'm 12, then again like I'm an A-level student." Two clear, accurate explanations in seconds — because explaining osmosis is a pattern it has seen thousands of times. This is the kind of thing it's genuinely great at.
✗ Where it lies to you "Give me three quotes from An Inspector Calls about social class." It may hand you two real quotes and one it invented — perfectly in style, sounds exactly like Priestley, doesn't exist in the play. Hand that in and your teacher will know in seconds.

Same tool, same confident tone in both. The difference isn't in how the answer looks — it's in what kind of question you asked. Patterns and explanations: usually solid. Specific facts, quotes, dates and numbers: check every single one.

4 Your turn

Catch the hallucination

Below are three answers in the style of an AI chatbot. Two are accurate. One contains a confident lie. Tap the one you think is hallucinating.

Answer A · asked about photosynthesis

"Photosynthesis is the process plants use to make glucose. Chlorophyll in the leaves absorbs light energy, which is used to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen."

✅ Accurate. Standard KS3/KS4 science — exactly the kind of well-worn pattern AI almost never gets wrong.
Answer B · asked about Macbeth

"Ambition is central to the play. In Act 1, Macbeth admits 'I have no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition', and later tells Lady Macbeth that 'ambition is a ladder to the stars, and I must climb it'."

🚨 Hallucination. The first quote is real. The second — "ambition is a ladder to the stars" — is completely invented. It sounds Shakespearean, it fits the theme, and it appears nowhere in the play. This is exactly how hallucinations work: one lie hidden among truths, in a perfect imitation of the right style.
Answer C · asked about World War One

"The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in June 1914 triggered a chain of events. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia, and the alliance system pulled in Russia, Germany, France and Britain within weeks."

✅ Accurate. Dates, places and sequence all check out.
The scary part: if you hadn't studied Macbeth, answer B would look identical to the real thing. That's the lesson — you can't spot a hallucination by how confident it sounds. You spot it by checking against something real: the book, your notes, your teacher.
5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

It's not your diary, and it's not your friend

Two things to lock in from day one:

  • What you type can be stored. On free chatbot accounts, your conversations may be kept and even used to train future versions. Treat every chat like a postcard, not a diary — never type in your full name, address, school, photos, passwords, or anything private about you or anyone else.
  • It sounds human. It isn't. It has no idea who you are, doesn't remember you between chats (unless it's been set up to), and doesn't care about you — it predicts words. Friendly tone is a pattern, not a feeling.

And the school line, stated plainly: AI tools stay off the school network and school devices. Everything in this module ran right here in your browser — no AI was needed, and none of these modules will ever ask you to use one.
