1 Why this matters

Everything you make with AI is harmless while it's on your screen. The moment you hit send — to one group chat, one story, one stream — you've published it, and publishing has rules that don't care how old you are or how funny it was meant to be.

Here's the mental shift this module asks for: stop thinking like someone posting and start thinking like someone publishing. A newspaper can't print whatever it likes about people. As of the moment you share, neither can you — and "I only sent it to the group chat" has never once stopped a screenshot.

2 The idea

Three questions before anything AI-made leaves your phone

1 · Is anyone real in it? Faces, voices, names, anything recognisable. If yes: did they actually say yes — not "they'd find it funny", but asked-and-agreed? Other people's identities are not raw material. You know the hard edge of this from E3: fake images of real people range from bullying to actual crime. The consent bar isn't "harmless" — it's "they agreed".
2 · Will people know it's AI? If your hyper-real image, voice or "news" clip travels without a label, you haven't shared content — you've launched misinformation, and you've become the exact thing E3 trained you to catch. The fix costs four characters: "(AI)". Label it where the label can't be cropped off.
3 · Would future-you sign it? Posts outlive moods, friendships and accounts. Sixth-form interviews, part-time jobs, eventually employers — people look, and screenshots don't expire. E2's screenshot test, upgraded for publishing: would I be comfortable with this on the form noticeboard, with my name on it, in five years?
And one for the forwarders: sharing someone else's post is publishing it — same three questions, even though you made none of it. "I just forwarded it" makes you the distributor, not a bystander. Every viral harm is mostly made of forwarders.
3 See it in action

Anatomy of a "harmless joke"

A made-up chain, assembled from very real patterns. A student makes an AI image of a teacher as a crying toddler in the lunch queue. Genuinely funny, honestly. Watch it travel:

7:42pmSent to the five-person group chat. Everyone laughs. Best case scenario — it could have ended here. It never ends here.
7:51pmOne of the five forwards it to a bigger chat — thirty people, half of whom don't know it's AI.
9:15pmIt's on three year-group stories. Someone's added the teacher's name. The "obviously a joke" context evaporated two hops ago.
TuesdayThe teacher sees it. They're a real person; it lands the way it would land on you. The school's behaviour policy covers conduct between members of the school community — including online, including made "as a joke", including AI.
WednesdayThe original maker is identified in about four minutes — originals always trace back. Now it's parents, sanctions, and a teacher who looks at them differently for a year. Cost of the joke: everything it touched. Value: eight seconds of group-chat laughter.

Run it through the three questions: real person — no consent (question 1: fail). That's all it took. The same student making the same joke about a fictional grumpy wizard teacher? Genuinely fine, genuinely funny, zero victims. The line was never the humour. It was the human.

4 Your turn

Post it or bin it?

Six things made at home, thumb hovering over send. Call each one.

An AI-generated fantasy landscape for the cover of your story, posted with "cover art I made with AI for chapter one!"

📤 Post it. No real people, clearly labelled, attached to work that's genuinely yours. This is exactly what sharing AI-assisted creativity should look like — all three questions pass without breaking stride.

An AI voice clip of your best mate "confessing" something embarrassing. He'd probably laugh. Probably.

🗑️ Bin it. Question 1 fails on the word "probably" — that's you guessing, not him agreeing. His voice is his. And once it's sent, E3 applies: nobody downstream knows it's fake, and "probably fine" has no edit button. If he'd really find it funny, show him in person and let him decide if it goes anywhere. (Spoiler: watch how fast "hilarious" changes when it's about to be published.)

A flawless AI "photo" of a shark in the flooded high street (E3 says hi), posted unlabelled "to see how many people fall for it".

🗑️ Bin it. Question 2, failed on purpose. "Social experiment" is what misinformation calls itself when it's local. You spent E3 learning to catch exactly this — making it makes you the thing you were inoculated against. Label it "(AI — gotcha test for my mates)" and it becomes a legitimate game instead.

Your short film for a youth competition — your script, your phone camera, AI background music, "Music: AI-generated" in the credits, and you checked the rules allow it.

📤 Post it — this is the gold standard. Real creative work (C1's director), honest credits, and the competition rules checked first rather than hoped about. That last step is the one most people skip; it's also the one that protects the whole entry.

An AI edit making a classmate look like he's being arrested. It's SO obviously fake. It's for the group chat. It's banter.

🗑️ Bin it — every word of that excuse is load-bearing and none of it holds. "Obviously fake" stops being obvious two forwards from now (see section 3). "Just the group chat" is where section 3 started. A fake image of a real classmate committing a crime is reputation damage with his face on it — bullying at best, and the school's policy will read it exactly that way. The wizard-teacher rule: make it about someone who doesn't exist.

An AI cartoon of your own dog as a knight, labelled, posted to your story.

📤 Post it. The dog's consent is assumed under the universal Good Boy doctrine. No humans, labelled, future-you would frame it. Go forth.

5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

The lines that carry legal weight

  • The E3 law line, one more time, because it belongs here too: sexual or sexualised fake images of anyone under 18 — making, having, or sharing, including of yourself, including "as a joke" — is a serious crime for everyone involved, forwarders included. There is no banter exception.
  • If something about you is out there: not your fault, fixable — evidence, trusted adult, Report Remove and CEOP. (E3 has the full kit; Childline 0800 1111 any time.)
  • School conduct reaches online conduct. What students publish about students or staff — on any app, made with any tool, at any hour — can land inside the behaviour policy. "It was AI" is not a defence; it's usually an aggravator.
  • The standing lines hold: no AI at school · nothing personal in the box — and other people's faces and voices count as personal, theirs.
