Homework, coursework and honesty

⏱ About 12 minutes🎯 Where the line actually is🚦 Six calls to make
1 Why this matters

"Is using AI on homework cheating?" is the wrong question — it's like asking "is using a calculator cheating?" In a mental-arithmetic test, yes. In an engineering exam, no. It depends what the work is for.

This module gives you the actual map: a traffic-light system for any piece of schoolwork, the test that tells you which light you're at, and the hard rules around GCSE and A-level coursework — where getting this wrong isn't a telling-off, it's the exam board's word for it: malpractice.

2 The idea

The purpose test, then the traffic light

Every task a teacher sets has a hidden second product. The visible one is the essay, the worksheet, the translation. The real one is the skill the task builds in you. So the test is:

The purpose test: what skill is this task supposed to build — and if AI does it, does that skill still get built in me? If the answer is no, the AI isn't helping you do the work. It's helping you not do it.

Run any AI use through that test and it lands on one of three lights:

🟢 Green — fine, no question The AI works on your understanding, not your answers: explaining things differently, quizzing you, giving feedback on a draft you wrote, building a revision plan. The skill still gets built — often faster. (All of T1 lives here.)
🟡 Amber — ask, and say what you did The grey zone: brainstorming angles, suggesting a structure, checking your grammar. Sometimes that's fine; sometimes generating the angles was the task. You usually can't tell from the outside — your teacher can. The amber rule: ask first if you can, and always be ready to say exactly what the AI did. If you'd rather not mention it, that's your conscience answering the question for you.
🔴 Red — never AI writes, solves or translates anything you submit with your name on it. Doesn't matter how much you "edited" it — if the thinking in the work isn't yours, the work isn't yours. For everyday homework that's dishonest and it sabotages you (E4). For GCSE/A-level coursework it's a different category entirely — see below.

The coursework rules are not school rules

For assessed coursework and NEA (non-exam assessment), the rules come from JCQ — the exam boards themselves. You sign a declaration that the work is your own. Submitting AI-written work as yours, or failing to acknowledge AI use where it was allowed, counts as malpractice — same category as plagiarism. Penalties run from losing the marks for that section, to losing the whole qualification. A teacher can't make that go away; it's above their heads.

And about getting caught: forget "AI detectors" — your teachers have something better. They've read your classwork all year. They know your voice. When paragraph three suddenly sounds like a corporate website, nobody needs software.

3 See it in action

One essay, three students, three lights

Year 11, English coursework-style essay on A Christmas Carol. Three students, three different evenings:

  • Student one (🟢): wrote her plan and draft herself; at home asked an AI to quiz her on Scrooge quotes until she knew them cold, then asked "what would a strict marker challenge in my argument?" — and fixed it herself. Every word hers, sharper than it would've been.
  • Student two (🟡 handled right): was stuck on structure, asked AI for three ways to organise an essay comparing Scrooge's past and present. Picked one, wrote everything himself — then told his teacher "I used AI to suggest structures, is that okay for this piece?" Teacher said fine, noted it. Done, honest, zero risk.
  • Student three (🔴): pasted the question, smoothed the output "into his own words", signed the declaration. The voice shift was obvious; the school had to treat it as suspected malpractice. Best case now: redo the work under supervision and a hard conversation with his parents. Worst case: it goes to the exam board.

Same essay title. The difference was never the tool — it was whose thinking ended up on the page, and whether they'd say so out loud.

4 Your turn

Call the light

Six situations, all at home on someone's own account. Green, amber or red?

"Quiz me on quadratic equations until I stop making sign errors — maths test Friday."

🟢 Green. Purpose test: the homework's skill is solving equations — and that skill is exactly what's being drilled into you. The AI never touches a submitted answer.

"Translate this paragraph into Spanish for my homework" — then copy it out neatly.

🔴 Red. The skill being built was translating. The AI built it for itself. Your teacher now plans next lesson believing you can do something you can't — so the help you need never arrives, and the speaking exam can't be outsourced.

"Give me five possible angles for my history essay on the causes of the Cold War — I'll choose one and write it all myself."

🟡 Amber. This is the classic grey zone: in some tasks, finding the angle is the skill being assessed. You can't tell from the outside — so ask, and be upfront about what the AI contributed. If you'd hide it, you already know.

"Write the analysis section of my geography NEA — I'll adjust the wording so it sounds like me."

🔴 Red — the most serious one on this page. NEA is exam-board assessed work with a signed declaration. "Adjusting the wording" changes nothing: the analysis isn't yours. This is JCQ malpractice territory, where the penalty can be the qualification itself.

"Here's the essay I've finished and my teacher's feedback from last time. Does this draft fix the problems she raised? Be specific."

🟢 Green. The work is done, by you. Using AI as a feedback mirror against your teacher's own criteria is the copilot pattern at its best — as long as you make the fixes. (One care from E2: the essay text itself is fine to share, but keep your name and school out of it.)

"Fix the spelling and grammar in my English homework before I hand it in."

🟡 Amber — and most people guess green. Purpose test: in English, spelling, grammar and expression are often part of what's being assessed and taught. Auto-fixing them hides exactly the errors your teacher needs to see to help you. Sometimes it's fine (a science write-up, maybe) — which is why it's an ask-first, not a free pass.

5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

The two questions that settle everything

  • The purpose test: "what skill is this building — and does it still get built in me?"
  • The daylight test (from E4): "could I tell my teacher exactly how I used AI on this, out loud, comfortably?" If yes, you're almost certainly fine. If no, stop.
  • For coursework and NEA, don't navigate it alone: the safe move is always to ask your teacher before using AI for anything connected to assessed work. They'd far rather answer that question than open a malpractice case.

And the standing lines: no AI at school, nothing personal in the box — your essays don't need your name attached to travel to a chatbot.
