Spot the fake

⏱ About 12 minutes🎯 A habit, not a superpower📱 Four posts to judge
1 Why this matters

A video of a celebrity saying something they never said. A "news photo" from a disaster that never happened. A voice note that sounds exactly like someone's mum. All of this is cheap to make now, and most of it is never labelled.

Here's the uncomfortable truth this module is built on: you are not going to out-stare a deepfake. The old advice — count the fingers, look at the eyes — worked for about a year. Modern AI images and video are often flawless. The people who don't get fooled aren't the ones with sharper eyes; they're the ones with a better habit.

2 The idea

Stop asking "does it look real?" Start asking "where's it from?"

Since you can't reliably judge the pixels, judge the provenance — the trail of where something came from. That's a four-step habit called SIFT, and it takes about 30 seconds:

S · Stop The moment something makes you feel a big emotion — outrage, fear, "no WAY" — that's the moment to pause. Strong emotion is exactly what fakes are engineered to trigger, because it makes you share before you think.
I · Investigate the source Who posted this? A news organisation with a reputation to lose, or an account called @truthbomb_2026 created last month? Tap the profile. Thirty seconds.
F · Find better coverage If a plane really crashed, every news outlet on Earth has it within minutes. If you can only find it on the one account that posted it — that silence is your answer.
T · Trace to the original Cropped screenshots and re-uploads hide context. Find where the clip or image first appeared and when. Old footage relabelled as "breaking news" is one of the most common fakes of all.
One more tell that still works: the too-perfect coincidence. The clip that confirms exactly what one side wants to believe, filmed at the perfect angle, surfacing at the perfect moment, from an account nobody's heard of? Reality is rarely that convenient.
3 See it in action

Thirty seconds on a viral clip

A clip is flying around: a shark swimming up a flooded high street, captioned "Storm Imogen hits Norfolk — UNREAL 😱". Two million views. Watch SIFT take it apart:

  • Stop: it's astonishing and scary — exactly the feeling that should slow you down, not speed you up.
  • Investigate: posted by a meme account that posts "unbelievable" clips daily. Not a news source, no location, no original claim of filming it.
  • Find better coverage: BBC and local Norfolk news cover the storm — flooding, school closures, no sharks. A two-million-view shark would be the headline. It isn't.
  • Trace: a reverse-image search finds the same shark, same angle, posted during a US hurricane years ago — it's a recycled fake that resurfaces in every flood, somewhere, every single year.

No pixel-staring required. The fake fell apart on source, coverage and history — things AI can't fake nearly as easily as faces.

4 Your turn

Trust it, or check it?

Four posts from a feed. You can't see the actual images — just like you can't see the truth behind a real post. Use the SIFT clues in each one and decide: seems trustworthy, or needs checking before you believe or share it?

⚡
@breaking_now_uk Account created 3 weeks ago · 112 followers

"🚨 LEAKED: footage of a Premier League star refusing to shake a kid's hand. Disgusting. RT before they take it down!!"

Video: blurry stadium tunnel, the star's face clearly visible, no date, no match named.
🔍 Check — and this one screams fake. Brand-new anonymous account, maximum-outrage caption, "before they take it down" urgency (a classic trick to stop you checking), and no date, match or source to verify. If it were real, sports media would have it everywhere within the hour. Searching for better coverage would settle it in seconds.
📰
BBC News Verified · posted 2 hours ago

"Flooding closes three schools in Hertfordshire after overnight storms. Live updates on road closures."

Photos: flooded roads, named locations, quotes from the county council. Same story on ITV News and the local paper.
✅ Trustworthy. Accountable source with a reputation to lose, specific checkable details, and — the big one — independent confirmation from multiple outlets. That's what real news looks like: boring, specific, and everywhere at once.
🎙️
Forwarded voice note via the group chat

"Listen to this — it's literally Mr [a teacher]'s voice slagging off Year 11. Someone recorded him!!"

Audio: sounds exactly like him. Forwarded four times; nobody knows who recorded it, where, or when.
🔍 Check — voices are the easiest thing to fake. A few seconds of someone's real voice is enough to clone it convincingly. No origin, no context, forwarded chain — that's all the warning you need. And think about the harm side: sharing it spreads what may be a fabricated attack on a real person. Don't forward it; flag it to an adult.
😢
@history_in_pics_daily "Unbelievable moments from history"

"A mother finds her son alive after the earthquake. The most powerful photo of the decade. 💔"

Photo: emotionally perfect composition, dramatic lighting, no photographer credit, no date, no location.
🔍 Check. Emotionally overwhelming + zero verifiable details (no credit, date or place) + an engagement-farming account = the classic profile of an AI-generated "history" photo. Real photojournalism comes with a photographer's name, an agency and a date. Reverse-image search would likely find nothing before last week.

5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

When the fake is of a person — it's serious

Everything above is about not being fooled. This part is about not being harmed, and not harming:

  • Making fake images of real people is never a joke. Using AI to put a classmate's face on another body, fake their voice, or make them "say" things is bullying at minimum — and if the image is sexual, it is a crime, full stop, including when the person making it is under 18. "It's AI, it's not really them" is not a defence in law or in life.
  • If it happens to you, it is not your fault — and it is fixable. Don't panic, don't negotiate with anyone threatening you, and don't delete everything: keep evidence (screenshots, usernames). Then tell an adult you trust — a parent/carer or any teacher.
  • There are real tools that work: Report Remove (Childline + the Internet Watch Foundation) can get sexual images of under-18s — including AI-made ones — taken down. Threats and pressure can be reported to CEOP. Childline (0800 1111) is free and confidential.
