Copilot, not pilot

⏱ About 10 minutes🎯 The principle behind everything✈️ Six flights to call
1 Why this matters

Here's the question nobody asks about AI doing your homework: if the AI does the learning, what exactly do you walk away with?

A copied essay gets you a grade — maybe — exactly once. But the grade was never the product. The brain that could write the essay is the product. That's what you take into the exam hall (handwritten, no AI, no exceptions), into interviews, into every job that's worth having in a world full of AI. This module is the one principle the whole of Marriotts runs on — staff included, in their own training: AI is the copilot. You are the pilot. Never the other way round.

2 The idea

Who's flying the plane?

In a cockpit, the pilot decides where to go, flies the plane, and is responsible for getting it down safely. The copilot assists: checks, challenges, suggests, catches mistakes. The copilot makes the pilot better — but the moment the copilot is flying and the pilot is asleep, that's not assistance any more. That's a passenger in a captain's hat.

Apply it to AI and the line becomes razor sharp:

  • Copilot uses (you're flying): "explain this until I get it", "quiz me", "I've written this — what's weak about it?", "give me three ways to think about this problem". The thinking happens in your head; the AI feeds it.
  • Pilot uses (it's flying): "write my essay", "do these questions", "just give me the answer". The thinking happens in the machine; you transcribe. You learned how to copy and paste, which you already knew.

And here's the part that's actually about your brain, not about rules: the struggle is the workout. The moment when an essay is hard, when you can't remember the quote, when the maths won't click — that effort is literally what builds the connections in your head. Scientists call it desirable difficulty. Skip the struggle and you skip the learning, the same way watching someone else do press-ups builds none of your muscle.

The one-question test: before you use AI for anything, ask — "after this, will I be able to do it without the AI?" If yes, it's a copilot use. If no — if you'd be helpless without it — it just flew the plane, and you were luggage.
3 See it in action

Same homework, two students

Two Year 10 students get the same English essay: "How does Priestley present responsibility in An Inspector Calls?" Both use AI at home. Watch the difference:

🧳 The passenger Pastes the question in. Copies the answer out. Changes a few words so it "sounds like him". Hands it in.

What he got: a paragraph his teacher instantly clocks as not-his-voice, a quote in it that doesn't exist (E1 says hi), zero revision value — and in the real exam, with no AI and a pen in his hand, nothing. The essay was never the point. The ability to write it was.
✈️ The pilot Writes her own plan and draft first. Then: "Quiz me on responsibility quotes until I know five cold." Then: "Here's my paragraph — what would a strict marker say is the weakest part?" Fixes it herself.

What she got: five quotes in her memory, a sharper argument in her own voice, and practice at exactly the skill the exam tests. The AI worked hard — but every bit of thinking happened in her head.

Same tool. Same evening. One of them gets better every time she does this; the other gets more dependent every time he does. That gap compounds for years.

4 Your turn

Pilot or passenger?

Six students using AI at home. For each: who's flying?

"Explain how electricity bills are calculated three different ways until one clicks — I don't get it from my notes."

✈️ Pilot. She's using AI as an unlimited-patience explainer to get something into her head. After this, she can do it without the AI — that's the test passed.

"Translate my French homework paragraph into French." Copies it out, hands it in.

🧳 Passenger. The AI did 100% of the French; he did 100% of the handwriting. His teacher now thinks he's better than he is — which means the help he actually needs never comes. And French exams are done with his brain only.

"I've finished my history answer. Act as a strict examiner — mark it against this list of things a top answer needs, and don't be nice."

✈️ Pilot — one of the best uses there is. The work is hers, done first. The AI is a feedback engine, and she decides what to do with the feedback. (One catch from E1: an "examiner" made of word-prediction can be confidently wrong — so weigh its feedback, don't obey it.)

"Write me a poem for the school competition. Something about the sea. Make it feel personal."

🧳 Passenger — and something worse. Entering AI work as your own creative work isn't just skipping a workout; it's claiming a voice that isn't yours. If "make it feel personal" has to go in the prompt, that's the giveaway. Competitions, like coursework, are about what you can make.

It's 11pm, the maths is due tomorrow. "Just give me the answers to these eight questions, quickly."

🧳 Passenger — the most tempting kind. Everyone has the 11pm night. But eight copied answers tell the teacher "he's got this" when he hasn't, and the gap shows up in the test where it costs most. The honest 11pm move: do what you can, and tell your teacher you got stuck on the rest. That message takes more courage than copying — and gets you actual help.

"Give me a plan for revising AQA Biology Paper 1 in three weeks — I'll tell you which topics I'm weakest on, you build the schedule."

✈️ Pilot. The AI is organising the work; she's still going to do the work — every hour of actual revision happens in her brain. Letting a copilot handle the admin so the pilot can fly is exactly the division of labour.

5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

The honesty line is also a legal-ish line

  • For GCSE and A-level coursework and assessments, the exam boards (JCQ) treat passing off AI work as your own as malpractice — the same category as copying from another student. It can mean losing marks for the piece or, in serious cases, the whole qualification. Not a detention. The qualification.
  • Exams are handwritten, no AI, no exceptions. Every passenger-mode shortcut between now and then is borrowing against the version of you who sits in that hall.
  • The simplest test of all: if you'd be uncomfortable telling your teacher exactly how you used AI on a piece of work, you already know which side of the line you're on.

And as always: none of this happens at school — AI stays off the school network and school devices — and nothing personal ever goes in the box.
