The rules — and the real risks

⏱ About 12 minutes🎯 Why the lines exist⚖️ Six judgement calls to make
1 Why this matters

"AI is banned at school" sounds like adults being adults. But the rules around AI aren't really about school — they're about you, and they keep applying when no one's watching.

This module gives it to you straight: what the school rule actually is and the real reasons behind it, what the law and the companies' own age limits say, and — most importantly — the lines you should draw for yourself, because they protect you everywhere, forever.

2 The idea

Two kinds of rules

1 · The school's line

No AI tools on the school network or school devices. Not because the school thinks AI is evil — the staff are literally being trained to use it — but because in school the school is legally responsible for you. That means it has to guarantee things no free chatbot can guarantee: that your personal data is protected, that what you see is appropriate, that everyone's work is genuinely theirs, and that tools meet age rules for every student in the room, Year 7 included.

2 · Your own lines

Outside school, the school can't make rules for you. But here's the thing the apps don't put on the tin: what you type into a chatbot can be stored, read by the company, and used to train future AI. On most free accounts, your chats are not private letters — they're more like postcards passing through a sorting office.

So the rule that protects you everywhere is the screenshot test:

The screenshot test: before you hit send, ask — "would I be okay if this exact message was screenshotted and shown to my form tutor, my parents, and my whole year group?" If not, it doesn't go in a chatbot. Ever.

Things that never pass the screenshot test: your full name + school + address, photos of yourself or friends, passwords, anything about your health or feelings you'd want kept private, and — this one matters — other people's private stuff. Your friend's secret is not yours to feed to a machine.

3 See it in action

How "just a chat" goes wrong

A made-up student — call her Maya, Year 10 — does three things that each feel harmless:

  • She tells a chatbot her full name, school and form group "so it can write a funny poem about her week".
  • She vents to it about a falling-out, naming the friend and what they did.
  • She uploads a group selfie and asks it to "make us look like anime characters".

Nothing explodes. Nothing ever seems to happen — and that's exactly the trap. Maya now has no idea where that information lives, who can see it, or what it trains. Her friend never agreed to be in a company's dataset. The photo of four people was uploaded with one person's consent.

Same fun, zero risk: "Write a funny poem about a Year 10 girl whose week included a soggy PE lesson and a maths test" — no name, no school. Vent about the falling-out to an actual human who cares. And the selfie stays out entirely — other people's faces are other people's property. The trick is almost never "don't do it"; it's "do it without handing over the personal bits".
4 Your turn

Would you type it?

Six messages about to be sent to a chatbot (at home, on someone's own account). For each one: fine to send, or never send?

"Explain Pythagoras' theorem like I'm terrible at maths, with a really simple example."

🟢 Fine. No personal information, nothing private — this is exactly the kind of use AI is good at. (Whether AI explaining beats AI doing your homework is module E4's whole point.)

"My friend Chloe in 9R told me she's been self-harming. What should I say to her?"

🔴 Never — twice over. It names Chloe and exposes her most private struggle to a company's servers. And this is far too important for a word-predictor: a situation like this needs a real adult — a parent, your head of year, or any teacher — today. Caring about your friend means telling a human.

"Quiz me on French food vocabulary, one question at a time, and keep score."

🟢 Fine. Zero personal data, and it's the AI working for your brain rather than instead of it.

"Here's a photo of my classmate — make it look like he's crying."

🔴 Never. His face, his consent — and he didn't give it. Editing images of real people to mock or embarrass them is bullying with extra steps, and depending on the edit it can cross into something illegal. Module E3 goes deeper on this.

"I'm [full name], I go to Marriotts in Stevenage and I live on [street]. Write my personal statement for sixth form."

🔴 Not like this. Fails the screenshot test: full identity package handed to a server. The task itself is fine if you strip the personal bits — "write a sixth-form personal statement for a Year 11 student who loves design and plays football" — then add your real details yourself, on your own device, afterwards. (And a personal statement should sound like you — see E4.)

"I've had this weird rash for two weeks, here's a photo — what is it and do I need a doctor?"

🔴 Never. Health stuff is the most private data you have, and a prediction machine is not a doctor — it can confidently reassure you about something serious, or terrify you about something harmless. Two weeks of a weird rash = tell a parent/carer and see a real professional.

5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

The lines, one more time

  • At school: no AI on the network or school devices. That's the policy and it protects everyone — including you.
  • Anywhere: nothing personal goes in the box. Not your details, not your photos, not your friends' secrets. Screenshot test, every time.
  • Age limits are real rules, not loading screens to skip. If you're under them, that's a conversation with a parent/carer, not a fake birthday.
  • Serious problems go to humans. Health, safety, friends in trouble — a chatbot is the wrong tool, every time. A parent/carer, any teacher, or Childline (0800 1111) is the right one.
