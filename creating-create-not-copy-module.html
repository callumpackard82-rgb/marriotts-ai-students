Create, don't copy

⏱ About 12 minutes🎯 Director, not vending machine🎬 Six creators to judge
1 Why this matters

AI can paint an image in ten seconds, write a song in thirty, and finish a story before you've found a pen. So here's the question every creative person your age has quietly asked: what's the point of me?

This module answers it. The short version: AI generates, but it doesn't care — it has no taste, no intentions, and nothing to say. Those are yours. The difference between people who are swallowed by these tools and people who are amplified by them is one mindset switch, and it's the whole of this module.

2 The idea

Director or vending machine?

There are two ways to use a creative AI, and they look similar from the outside:

  • The vending machine. 🥤 Type "cool dragon wallpaper", take what drops out, slap your name on it. No idea of your own went in; no skill of yours came out. You didn't make anything — you ordered something. (E1 explains what you ordered: the average of everything the model has seen. Generic by definition.)
  • The director. 🎬 You arrive with an idea — a mood, a story, a sound in your head. The AI becomes a tireless assistant: you try versions, reject most, push it somewhere specific, combine its output with work that's genuinely yours, and the final thing matches your intention. The taste, the decisions, the point of it all — those stayed in your chair.
The test (you know it from E4): who did the creative thinking? A director can explain every choice in the finished piece — why this colour, why this line, why not the first ten versions. A vending-machine user can only explain what they typed. If there are no decisions to explain, there's no creation to claim.

Whose name goes on it?

  • Honesty rule: if AI made it or heavily shaped it, say so — "made with AI" costs you nothing; getting caught pretending costs you everything (and you will get caught — pure AI output has a recognisable flavour of generic).
  • Schoolwork and competitions: art, music, DT and creative-writing coursework follow the same JCQ malpractice rules as essays (T2) — and most competitions ban or require disclosure of AI work. Entering the vending machine's output under your name is the creative version of the passenger from E4, with the same consequences.
  • Other creators are real people: these models learned from millions of artists' and writers' work, mostly without their say — which is why many artists are angry, and why "make it in the style of [living artist]" then posting it as your sound or style is legal grey area and ethical red. Borrow from the dead and the willing; develop your own voice with the living.
3 See it in action

Two posters for the school production

Drama department needs a poster for Blood Brothers. Two volunteers, both using AI at home:

🥤 The vending machine Types "Blood Brothers poster dramatic". Takes the second result. It's… fine. It's also exactly what anyone on Earth would get from the same prompt: generic theatrical figures, meaningless shadows, text that needs fixing anyway.

What it says: nothing. About the play, or them.
🎬 The director Starts with her own idea: two identical paper dolls, one cut from a bank note, one from a bus ticket — the play's whole class divide in one image. Uses AI to explore moods and layouts, rejects fifteen versions, sketches the final concept herself, photographs real paper cut-outs, uses an AI tool only to clean up the background — and credits it.

What it says: she understood the play. The idea was never in the machine. It was in her.

Same tool, same evening, same play. One poster is a product of a prompt; the other is a product of a person — and everyone who sees them can tell.

4 Your turn

Director or vending machine?

Six creators, all working at home. Call each one.

Types "epic wolf digital art 4k", posts the first output with the caption "my new piece 🎨".

🥤 Vending machine — twice over. No idea went in, and "my new piece" is a lie on top. Post it as "AI wolf I generated, isn't it cool" and it's harmless fun. The caption is the difference between sharing and stealing credit from a machine.

Designs her own comic character, then uses AI to try ten colour palettes on her sketch before painting the final version herself.

🎬 Director. The character, the sketch, the final paint — hers. The AI did what assistants do: fast experiments she judged. Every choice in the finished piece is one she can explain.

Art homework due tomorrow: generates thirty images, picks the least obviously AI one, hands it in.

🥤 Vending machine — and worse, it's submitted work. Picking from thirty random outputs isn't curation, it's a fruit machine. And handing it in crosses from lazy into dishonest (T2's red light) — in assessed art coursework, into malpractice.

Writes his own song lyrics, asks AI "where does the rhythm stumble? don't rewrite anything — just point", then fixes the lines himself.

🎬 Director. Notice the instruction: don't rewrite — point. That's a creator protecting their own voice while taking feedback. The lyrics that come out the other end are still 100% his, just better.

"Make me a track that sounds exactly like [a current chart artist]" — uploads it to streaming as his own sound.

🥤 Vending machine, plus an ethics problem. The "sound" he's claiming is a living artist's, laundered through a model. It's the least creative act on this page: someone else's voice, a machine's execution, his name. Building your sound is the entire job of being an artist — there's no shortcut to having something to say.

Finishes writing her short story, then asks AI for ten title ideas — hates all of them, but one sparks a better idea of her own.

🎬 Director — the everyday kind. The story is hers; the AI was a brainstorm partner whose ideas she rejected on taste. "It sparked a better idea of my own" is maybe the single healthiest sentence in all of AI-assisted creativity.

5 🛡️ Safety checkpoint

Hard lines in creative AI

  • Never generate images of real people. Not classmates, not teachers, not celebrities, not "just as a joke". You know the law from E3 — and even the legal kinds are someone's face used without their consent. Fictional characters and invented people: go wild.
  • Label what's AI-made whenever you share it. Unlabelled AI passed off as real is how you become the fake that E3 taught you to catch.
  • Assessed creative work follows T2's rules: if AI touched coursework, your teacher needs to know — before, not after.
  • And always: no AI at school · nothing personal in the box — your prompts don't need your name, school or photos of anyone real.
